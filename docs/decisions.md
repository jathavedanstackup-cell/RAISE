## Allergy flag: passive display vs. staff acknowledgment
- **Date**: 2026-09-21
- **Affects checkpoint(s)**: CP8 (kitchen display) -- this is CP8's own gate, separate from the accept-step entry below. An earlier read treated the accept-step decision as unblocking CP8; it does not. CP8 names two prerequisites: CP6 merged, *and* this entry filled in.
- **Status**: **DECIDED** by Jathavedan, 2026-09-21. Not provisional -- see the note on evidence below.
- **Decision**: **Acknowledgment required, and the flag never recedes.** A ticket carrying an allergy flag cannot be marked `food_out` until a staff member explicitly acknowledges the flag. The acknowledgment is recorded as an auditable event. It does **not** change what is rendered: the flag keeps exactly the same prominence, treatment and position before and after. Acknowledgment records that a human looked; it is not a dismissal.
- **Why**:
  - **The disappearing-flag failure mode is the thing to design against.** The conventional acknowledgment pattern dismisses or quiets what it acknowledges -- that is what makes it feel like progress. Applied to an allergy, it produces a ticket that *looks clean* precisely when it is most dangerous: the plater who picks it up after the acknowledger is now working from a screen that no longer warns them. A flag that is never asked about at all is safer than one that vanishes on tap. So the acknowledgment and the display are deliberately decoupled: one is an event, the other is a permanent property of the ticket.
  - **Reversibility asymmetry, the same test used for the accept-step below.** Removing an acknowledgment control later is deleting a control and stopping writes to an event table. Adding one later means a new UI surface, a new state transition, a new audit record, and a migration -- retrofitted onto a kitchen display built assuming none of it existed. The expensive direction is the one to take now, while it is cheap.
  - **Consistency with the trust boundaries CP5/CP6/CP7 established.** This codebase's standing position is that consequential real-world actions require an explicit human affirmative step, and that the step leaves an audit record. Food reaching a guest with a declared allergy is the most consequential action in the product. Treating it as the one place where display-without-confirmation is sufficient would contradict the precedent every prior checkpoint has set.
  - **Honest counter-argument, recorded rather than hidden**: this adds a tap at the busiest moment, and a kitchen under rush pressure may acknowledge reflexively without reading -- the same rubber-stamp risk logged against the accept-step below. That risk is real and accepted. The mitigation is *not* more friction; it is that the flag stays visible afterwards, so a reflexive tap costs the audit trail's meaning but not the warning itself. This is the specific reason the never-dismiss half is load-bearing and not decoration.
- **Safety-flag visual treatment** (resolves CP8's "design input" note and `docs/concept-critique.md`'s double-duty accent finding): allergy flags do **not** share the accent colour used for priority/next. The deck reuses one accent for both, which means the most urgent signal on the screen is indistinguishable from a routine one. Safety flags keep the existing high-contrast chip treatment already shipped in `apps/restaurant` (`--chip-allergen-bg` / `--chip-allergen-fg`), and must remain identifiable **without relying on colour alone** -- a sun-washed pass screen and a colour-blind cook are both ordinary conditions, not edge cases. Priority/next may keep the accent; allergy may not.
- **On evidence**: the original gate said "pending restaurant interviews." Those have not happened. This is decided from the reversibility argument rather than from field evidence, and is marked DECIDED rather than PROVISIONAL because the never-dismiss property should not be quietly traded away later for screen tidiness. If interviews produce evidence that the acknowledgment step is pure friction, the *acknowledgment* may be dropped. The permanent-prominence rule stays regardless.

## Kitchen auto-start vs. staff accept-step
- **Date**: 2026-09-19
- **Affects checkpoint(s)**: CP6 (timing engine), CP8 (kitchen display — shares this decision, per `docs/execution-guide.md` §4)
- **Status**: **PROVISIONAL** — revisit trigger named below. This is not the final answer, it's the answer that lets CP6/CP8 proceed without guessing.
- **Decision**: Staff accept-step. The computed `kitchen_start_time` raises a prompt for kitchen staff; prep timing only counts as started once a staff member explicitly accepts it. The system never auto-fires `kitchen_started` on its own.
- **Why** (evidence, not just preference):
  - **Reversibility asymmetry is the deciding factor, not a guess at kitchen preference.** Going from staff-accept to auto-start later is a feature-flag flip — remove the prompt, let the computed time write the status directly. Going the other way — discovering after CP8 ships that staff actually wanted a checkpoint — means retrofitting a UI surface, a notification path, and a new state transition onto a kitchen display that was built assuming none of that existed. One direction is cheap to undo; the other isn't. Absent real evidence yet, the cheap-to-reverse option is the correct default.
  - **Consistency with RAISE's own established stance.** Part 5 of the production plan, and what CP5 just spent an entire checkpoint enforcing as a hard trust boundary, is that nothing consequential happens without an explicit human affirmative action — a customer's booking doesn't confirm itself on a computed timestamp, a person taps "confirm." Auto-starting the kitchen on a computed `kitchen_start_time` is the same category of action — a system-computed instant triggering a real-world consequence — with the human step removed. Staff accept-step keeps CP6 consistent with the precedent CP5 just set, rather than quietly contradicting it one checkpoint later.
  - **Honest counter-argument, recorded rather than hidden**: the accept-step adds friction at exactly the moment speed is the entire point of the product — a five-minute head start on prep is the deck's whole pitch. A kitchen under dinner-rush pressure may tap "accept" reflexively without actually checking the ticket, which buys none of the safety the checkpoint intends and only costs the friction. This is a real, accepted cost, not an oversight.
  - **PROVISIONAL, with a named revisit trigger**: after this has run in 10 real services, if staff accept more than 95% of prompts without modifying the timing (i.e., the accept step is consistently a rubber stamp, not a real check), auto-start should be revisited as the better default — the evidence at that point would say the human step isn't catching anything the computed time doesn't already get right. Until that evidence exists, staff accept-step stands.
- **Unblocks**: CP6 may start now. CP8 is also unblocked by this same entry, per its own gate note.

### The formula: what's given, what's derived, what's new
`kitchen_start_target = arrival_eta - max(prepTimeMinutes) - avgPrepBufferMinutes` is not a CP6 invention — `docs/production-plan.md` Part 3 states it literally, and `avgPrepBufferMinutes` (`5`) has been sitting in `Restaurant.settings` since CP1's seed data specifically so CP6 would have it ready.

`food_out_target` has no formula anywhere in the docs. The first thing checked was whether the deck's numbers even admit a formula that reuses the same buffer symmetrically (`food_out = kitchen_start + prep + avgPrepBufferMinutes`) — they don't, and not by a rounding artifact: that shape algebraically collapses to `food_out = arrival_eta` exactly, for any split of prep/buffer, because `kitchen_start + prep + buffer` is just `arrival_eta` restated. The deck's food_out (8:18) is 3 minutes after arrival (8:15), not equal to it, so a second, independent constant is structurally required — not a numbers problem, a shape problem. `Restaurant.settings` gained a new key, `expoBufferMinutes`, for exactly this (flagged per Rule #4 in `schema.prisma`'s own comment). With the existing `avgPrepBufferMinutes=5` and CP1's own seeded 12-minute dish, `expoBufferMinutes=8` reconciles the deck's example exactly: `food_out_target = kitchen_start_target + prep + expoBufferMinutes`. `timing-engine.spec.ts` states this derivation inline rather than hiding it behind numbers chosen to make the assertion pass.

### Timezone / DST
Every timing value is an instant (`Date`/UTC epoch); every calculation is instant arithmetic (`getTime() ± minutes*60_000`), never a parse or format of a local wall-clock string. That is what makes it DST-safe — a DST transition changes how an instant *displays* in a timezone, never how many real minutes sit between two instants. `timing-engine.ts` never touches `Restaurant.timezone` at all for this reason. `timing-engine.spec.ts`'s DST test proves this concretely against the actual 2027 US spring-forward boundary: a naive local-time-string subtraction would compute a wall-clock time that never existed that morning (2:00-2:59 AM is skipped); this engine can't produce that, because it never manipulates local-time strings in the first place. Rendering these instants in the restaurant's own local time (for a screen a person reads) is explicitly CP8's job, using the same instant + `Intl.DateTimeFormat(restaurant.timezone, ...)` approach, never local-time arithmetic.

### Recompute-on-drift: the same concurrency mechanism CP5 used, not a new one
Several ETA updates racing is the same shape as two guests racing for the last table — CP5's fix (an atomic conditional `UPDATE`, not read-then-write) is reused directly, keyed on the visit's own `updatedAt` as an optimistic-concurrency token instead of `status`. A stale request (built against an `updatedAt` the row has since moved past) is rejected outright rather than silently overwriting a fresher state. Proven red/green: the optimistic-lock condition was temporarily dropped, the "rapid succession" test failed with the exact predicted symptom (the stale second update reported `applied: true` instead of being rejected), then restored and reverified green.

"You cannot un-cook food": once a visit has left `confirmed` (kitchen already accepted, or later), `recomputeForEtaChange`/`recompute` are no-ops on the targets — they return `visit_not_confirmed` rather than silently rewriting instants that are now historical facts, not predictions. Tested directly (drift attempted after a real accept via the HTTP endpoint; targets and the visit's own `arrivalEta` are verified byte-for-byte untouched).

### The accept-step trust boundary
`TimingService.acceptKitchenStart` is the only method anywhere that writes `Visit.status = 'kitchen_started'`, proven with the same static source-scan invariant pattern `visits.e2e-spec.ts` established for `confirmed` — and in proving it, that original CP5 regex turned out to be too broad (see Finding below), fixed here rather than routed around.

`awaiting_accept` from the requested state machine (`confirmed → awaiting_accept → kitchen_started`) is deliberately **not** a persisted `VisitStatus` enum value. It's a derived read-time condition (`status = 'confirmed' AND kitchenStartTarget <= now()`), not a state a background job has to write — there's no scheduler/poller in this checkpoint's scope, and none is needed: the prompt condition is knowable from data already on the row. `interaction-design:state-machine`'s own guard/state distinction is exactly this — a guard gates a transition, it isn't itself a state. Flagged explicitly since it's a deliberate simplification against the literal wording of the build brief, not an oversight.

### Realtime event contract
`visit.timing.recomputed` / `visit.kitchen.accepted`, emitted via `EventEmitter2` per NestJS's own documented pattern (confirmed live via Context7, since no installed skill covers this). In-process only — no WebSocket Gateway or SSE endpoint is built here; that's explicitly CP7/CP8's job when something needs to relay these across the wire.

### Deliberately out of scope, flagged rather than silently gapped
- **No automatic trigger wires the first computation into CP5's `confirm()` flow.** `visits.service.ts` — CP5's shipped, fully-reviewed, trust-boundary-critical file — is untouched by this checkpoint except where its own test needed the regex fix below. `TimingService.recompute` is idempotent and safe to call any time a confirmed visit has a table, an ETA, and at least one item (including as the very first call), exposed via `POST .../timing/recompute`; wiring an automatic call is a one-line follow-up for whichever checkpoint first has a natural caller (CP7's dashboard, most likely).
- **No new customer-facing "change my ETA" endpoint.** The checkpoint names "recompute on drift when arrival_eta changes" as an engine capability, but no UX for a guest to actually request that change exists anywhere in the docs (call the restaurant? text? nothing defines this). Building a full endpoint/guard/frontend for an unspecified flow would have been scope invention, not scope fulfillment. `recomputeForEtaChange` is fully built and tested at the service level, ready for whichever checkpoint defines that UX.

### Finding: CP5's own TRUST BOUNDARY test was too broad
CP6 is the first other code that legitimately *checks* `status = 'confirmed'` (a `where:` read-guard — "only touch rows still in this state," the same precondition style CP5 itself uses for its own atomic claims) without writing it. CP5's original regex (`/status:\s*['"]confirmed['"]/`) matched that read-guard exactly as readily as an actual write, and flagged `timing.service.ts` as a false-positive violation. Fixed in `visits.e2e-spec.ts` to scope the match to Prisma's `data:` write-argument shape specifically, preserving the test's actual intent. Re-verified red/green after the fix: a genuine write-shaped violation (`data: { status: 'confirmed' }`) planted in `timing.service.ts` was still caught by the corrected regex, then reverted, then confirmed green — the fix narrows false positives without weakening what the test actually guards.

## CP7 — realtime WebSocket authentication: ticket-exchange, not a raw JWT
- **Date**: 2026-09-20
- **Affects checkpoint(s)**: CP7 (FOH dashboard), and CP8 (kitchen display) which inherits this same mechanism for its own realtime feed
- **Decision**: A browser-initiated WebSocket authenticates via a short-lived, single-use **connect ticket**, never the long-lived staff JWT itself.
  1. `apps/restaurant` (server-side, same `apiFetch`/httpOnly-cookie boundary `session.ts`/`api-client.ts` already use) calls a new `POST /realtime/ticket` on `apps/api`, authenticated with the staff JWT exactly like every other existing call.
  2. `apps/api` verifies the caller via the existing `StaffJwtGuard`/`StaffRestaurantGuard` chain (unchanged), then mints a ticket: a random opaque value, TTL ~20s, single-use, scoped to `{ staffUserId, restaurantId }`, held in an in-memory store (see scope note below).
  3. The Server Action hands the browser **only the ticket** — the 12h staff JWT never leaves `apps/restaurant`'s server runtime at any point in this flow.
  4. The browser opens the WebSocket carrying the ticket via the `Sec-WebSocket-Protocol` header, not a URL query string — the same reasoning as CP4's IDOR fix: anything in a URL lands in proxy/access logs and browser history, and a WS handshake URL is no exception.
  5. The Gateway's `handleConnection` verifies and immediately invalidates the ticket (replay protection), then joins the socket to a **server-computed** room (`restaurant:${restaurantId}`, taken from the ticket's own claim) — never a room the client requests.
- **Why** (evidence, not just preference):
  - This is RAISE's first realtime surface anywhere in the codebase — no installed skill covers NestJS WebSocket Gateway auth specifically (checked the filesystem skill list directly, same Rule #1 discipline as CP6's event-emitter lookup), so the design was grounded in a proportionate threat-model pass against this codebase's actual files (`session.ts`, `api-client.ts`, `tenant-prisma.service.ts`, CP2's tenant-isolation entry, CP4's IDOR postmortem) rather than a generic web-app checklist.
  - **Two P0 findings drove the two non-negotiables**: (1) a bare `server.emit()` on a single shared multi-tenant process would broadcast one restaurant's live visit/allergy/table data to every other connected restaurant's staff — a direct cross-tenant disclosure, not a theoretical one, given the process topology. (2) trusting a client-supplied `restaurantId` on any subscribe/join message is exactly the same trust-boundary shape as CP4's original IDOR (identity taken from user input instead of a verified token). Both are structural rules for the implementation, not suggestions.
  - **Why a ticket and not the JWT directly**: a raw WebSocket handshake has no clean way to carry a bearer `Authorization` header the way `fetch()` does, and exposing the 12h staff JWT to client-side JS to work around that would break the httpOnly boundary `session.ts` was deliberately built around. A short-lived, single-use, narrowly-scoped ticket is the same "capability token, not identity token" shape already established for CP4's intake draft token and CP5's customer token — reused, not invented fresh.
  - **Membership can change mid-connection** (a `StaffMembership` role edit or removal, already possible since CP2) with no natural re-check point the way each HTTP request gets one for free via `StaffJwtGuard`. Chose a short ticket TTL forcing periodic reconnection over building a second revocation mechanism — it inherits CP2's existing enforcement instead of duplicating it, proportionate to "one new surface," not a rebuild of auth.
  - **In-memory ticket store, not Redis**: tickets are single-process, ~20s-lived, and this codebase has no Redis dependency anywhere yet. Adding one now for a 20-second value would be reaching for infrastructure the project doesn't otherwise need. Flagged explicitly: this stops being correct the moment `apps/api` runs as more than one process/instance (a ticket minted on instance A wouldn't be recognized by instance B) — fine for this project's current single-instance deployment, a real constraint to revisit before any horizontal scaling.
  - Every DB read inside a Gateway handler still goes through `TenantPrismaService.forRestaurant`, called fresh per operation — never a Prisma client scoped once and cached on the socket for its lifetime, which would silently reintroduce the same class of bypass CP2 deliberately deleted (`bypassScope`) before merge.
## ORM / migration tool: Prisma (v7, pinned)
- **Date**: 2026-09-13
- **Affects checkpoint(s)**: CP1 (and every checkpoint that touches the schema after)
- **Decision**: Use Prisma ORM for the data model, migrations, and seed script. Pin `prisma` and `@prisma/client` to matching `^7.10.0` — not `latest`.
- **Why** (evidence, not just preference):
  - Checked what's actually available before choosing (per CLAUDE.md Rule #1) rather than defaulting to whichever ORM is most familiar:
    - `npx skills find prisma` (via the Vercel Skills reference repo) surfaces `prisma/skills` — an **official**, actively maintained skill suite (`prisma-postgres-setup`, `prisma-cli`, `prisma-client-api`, `prisma-driver-adapter-implementation`, …) each with 265K–282K installs.
    - `npx skills find drizzle` surfaces only third-party community skills, the best at ~4.8K installs — no official `drizzle-team` skill exists. Per the find-skills quality bar (prefer 1K+ installs and official sources), Prisma has a clear, verifiable edge here, not just a popularity impression.

Drizzle is lighter/closer to raw SQL; Prisma's declarative `schema.prisma` + relations map directly onto Part 3's FK-heavy shape (Restaurant → MenuItem/Table/Visit → VisitItem/ConversationTurn/NotificationLog), and its built-in `db seed` convention is a direct fit for CP1's "seed script with one demo restaurant" deliverable without extra tooling.

Installing "latest" naively would silently pull a mismatched, pre-release CLI against a stable client.   - Integration pattern: a hand-written `PrismaService` (extends `PrismaClient`, implements `OnModuleInit`/`OnModuleDestroy`) per NestJS's own official docs, not the third-party `nestjs-prisma` wrapper package — no need for the extra dependency at this scale.

## CP1 schema shape: deviations from Part 3's literal sketch, flagged per Rule #4
- **Date**: 2026-09-13
- **Affects checkpoint(s)**: CP1 (schema), CP2 (tenant isolation), CP6 (timing engine), CP8 (kitchen display)
- **Decision**: Three modelling choices go beyond, or diverge from, Part 3's literal ASCII schema. Flagging explicitly rather than deciding silently, per CLAUDE.md Rule #4 and this checkpoint's own instruction:
  1. **`restaurant_id` denormalized onto every child table**, not just the tables Part 3 lists it on directly. Part 3 only shows `restaurant_id` on `MenuItem`/`Table`/`Visit`; `VisitItem`, `ConversationTurn`, `NotificationLog`, and `VisitStatusEvent` (below) got it too, plus a matching `@@index([restaurantId])`.
  2. **`VisitStatusEvent` — a new, separate append-only table** for `Visit.status` transitions, rather than more flat timestamp columns on `Visit` (Part 3 shows `arrival_confirmed_at`/`table_assigned_at` as flat columns and only says "timestamps for every status transition" in prose, without picking a shape). The CP1 prompt explicitly allowed either ("first-class column/row"); chose the table because it's genuinely append-only (a status can be revisited without a migration) and because CP6's "recompute kitchen_start_time on ETA drift" is naturally "insert another event," not "overwrite a column."
  3. **`VisitItem.modifications` modeled as fully structured (`String[]`), not "free-form + structured tags"** as Part 3's prose literally says. The CP1 prompt's own instruction ("Allergens and modifications are STRUCTURED tags, not free text") is more specific and more recent than Part 3's general description, so it took precedence per CLAUDE.md's authority order (explicit task instructions over project docs). There is currently no free-text notes field on `VisitItem` — if a later checkpoint needs one (e.g. a customer's genuinely unstructured aside), that's a deliberate addition then, not an oversight now.
- **Why**: (1) is the concrete mechanism CP2's tenant-isolation checks (app-level guards and/or Postgres RLS) need — without it, isolating `VisitItem`/`ConversationTurn`/etc. requires a join back through `Visit` on every check, which is slower and awkward for RLS `USING` clauses. (2) and (3) are both about keeping the kitchen-facing data (timing history, allergy/modification flags) structured and machine-renderable rather than prose, which is the explicit point of CP6 and CP8.

## CP2: auth primitives — do not hand-roll
- **Date**: 2026-09-14
- **Affects checkpoint(s)**: CP2 and everything built on staff/customer auth after it
- **Decision**: Password hashing via `argon2` (argon2id). JWT issuance/verification via `@nestjs/jwt`'s `JwtService`, through a hand-rolled `CanActivate` guard (not `passport-jwt`). Phone OTP via Twilio Verify in production, behind an `OtpProvider` interface with a dev-only in-memory stub selected by `OTP_PROVIDER` (default `dev`).
- **Why** (evidence, not just preference):
  - Checked for a fitting skill first, per CLAUDE.md Rule #1: neither the local Vercel Skills repo (`C:\AI-References\skills`) nor `npx skills find` turned up anything for NestJS auth, JWT, or OTP clearing the "1K+ installs, reputable source" bar the repo's own reference-repo guidance sets — best hits were low-install community skills or unrelated pentest/exploit skills (e.g. JWT algorithm-confusion attack skills, not implementation guidance). Falling back to official docs + established libraries, as that same guidance's own fallback path specifies.
  - **Argon2id, not bcrypt**: OWASP's current Password Storage Cheat Sheet recommends Argon2id as the first choice; the `argon2` npm package is actively maintained and its native binding was verified to build and round-trip correctly in this environment before adopting it.
  - **Hand-rolled `CanActivate` guard over `passport-jwt`**: confirmed via Context7 (`/nestjs/jwt`) that NestJS's own current JWT documentation demonstrates exactly this shape — `JwtService.sign`/`.verify` inside a custom guard — without requiring the `passport`/`passport-jwt` dependency chain. Fewer moving parts for the same officially-documented pattern.
  - **Twilio Verify, not the raw Messaging API or a hand-rolled code table**: Verify owns code generation, expiry, resend cooldowns, and brute-force/fraud detection server-side — confirmed via Context7 (`/twilio/twilio-node`) and Twilio's own `twilio/ai@twilio-migrate-messaging-to-verify` skill (found via `npx skills find`, low install count but from Twilio itself), which exists specifically to move people off hand-rolled SMS-code flows and onto Verify. Hand-rolling OTP generation/storage/rate-limiting would be exactly the kind of security-critical primitive Rule #1 says not to freehand.
  - **Separate signing secrets per audience** (`JWT_STAFF_SECRET` / `JWT_CUSTOMER_SECRET`), not one secret plus an `aud` claim each side checks: a leak of the customer-facing secret (a much larger, lower-trust surface — public signup, phone OTP) cannot be used to forge a staff token, and vice versa. This is enforced by `jsonwebtoken`'s own `audience` sign/verify options, not a manual field comparison.

## CP2 Part 8 Q4: phone/OTP provider and guest-first policy
- **Date**: 2026-09-14
- **Affects checkpoint(s)**: CP2 (auth), CP4/CP5 (customer flow assumes a session already exists)
- **Decision**: Twilio Verify is the production phone-OTP provider. **No full account is required before a first booking** — guest-first, per the deck's "you just speak" framing. A verified phone number alone creates a `Customer` row (`phone`, `phoneVerifiedAt`) and issues a customer session token; `name`/`email` stay nullable and are collected later, never gating the first visit.
- **Why**:
  - The deck's explicit selling point is frictionless intake; gating a first booking behind full account creation (password, email verification, etc.) would reintroduce the exact friction RAISE exists to remove.
  - Twilio Verify (see the primitives decision above) was chosen for the same "don't hand-roll" reasoning, and its API shape (`Verifications.create` / `VerificationChecks.create`, keyed by phone number, not by an existing account) fits a guest-first flow naturally — there is no "create account, then verify" step to skip around.
  - Dev-mode stub (`OTP_PROVIDER=dev`, the default): an in-memory `DevOtpProvider` that never calls Twilio, never requires `TWILIO_*` credentials, and logs the code instead of sending it. `OTP_DEV_FIXED_CODE` makes it deterministic for CI. This is what local dev and the CI isolation test both run against — no SMS credits burned, no live provider keys required for `main` to stay green.

## CP2 tenant-isolation enforcement layer: Postgres RLS + Prisma Client Extension, both in depth

- **Affects checkpoint(s)**: CP2 and every checkpoint touching restaurant-scoped data after it (CP3, CP6, CP7, CP8, ...)
- **Decision**: Both mechanisms, not one or the other:
  1. **Postgres Row Level Security** on every restaurant-scoped table (`restaurants`, `menu_items`, `tables`, `visits`, `visit_status_events`, `visit_items`, `conversation_turns`, `notification_logs`, `staff_memberships`), with `FORCE ROW LEVEL SECURITY` so even the owning role is subject to it. Policy: a row is visible/writable only if `restaurant_id = current_setting('app.current_restaurant_id', true)`. **No bypass policy.** An earlier version of this migration also had a permissive `tenant_bypass` policy (`current_setting('app.bypass_rls', true) = 'on'`) and a matching `TenantPrismaService.bypassScope()` method, meant for seeding / a future platform-admin path. It was removed in a pre-merge security fix: Postgres ORs permissive policies together, so that policy meant any code path able to set `app.bypass_rls` disabled tenant isolation on **every** table, for reads and writes, everywhere — and it was uncalled dead code besides, since seeding already runs as the superuser `DATABASE_URL` role, which bypasses RLS structurally and never needed a policy-level escape hatch. If a genuinely cross-tenant platform-admin path is needed later (CP11+), it gets its own narrowly-scoped mechanism then, with a test pinning its blast radius — not a standing "disable isolation" switch kept around unused in case something someday wants it.
  2. **A Prisma Client Extension** (`TenantPrismaService.forRestaurant(id, ...)`), following Prisma's own documented pattern (confirmed via Context7 `/prisma/web`), that wraps each request's queries in a transaction and sets that session variable via `set_config` before running them.
  3. **A second, unprivileged Postgres role (`raise_app`)** created by this migration for the app's *runtime* connection (`APP_DATABASE_URL`), distinct from the migration/seed role (`DATABASE_URL`).
- **Why**: the checkpoint's own instruction was explicit — "a rule that must be remembered by every future developer on every future query is not an enforcement layer; prefer the mechanism that fails closed by default." An app-level `WHERE restaurantId = ...` guard alone is exactly that forgettable rule: one missed clause in one future query and it's a leak. RLS is what makes the isolation hold even if that happens.
  - **The second role is not optional, and was not obvious going in.** Before writing any policy, I checked the actual local Postgres role used for migrations (`raise`) and found `rolsuper=t, rolbypassrls=t` — it's a superuser, because the Docker Postgres image's `POSTGRES_USER` bootstrap account always is, in both local dev and this project's CI service container. Superusers bypass RLS unconditionally, regardless of `FORCE ROW LEVEL SECURITY` — so if the running API had kept connecting as `raise`, every policy in this migration would have been silently inert, and the isolation test would only have been passing because of the application-level `WHERE` clauses it's specifically meant not to depend on. Verified the fix directly with `psql` before writing any application code: as `raise_app` with no session variable set, a known-existing row returns zero results; with the matching `app.current_restaurant_id` set, it returns exactly that row.
  - Flagging per CLAUDE.md Rule #4: this DB-role split is new infrastructure this checkpoint introduces, not something CP0/CP1 anticipated. `docker-compose.yml` needed no changes (the role is created by a migration, not compose), but `.env`/`.env.example`/CI/`turbo.json` all needed a new `APP_DATABASE_URL` alongside `DATABASE_URL`.
  - **Known limitation, explicitly not swept under the rug**: the migration creates `raise_app` with a fixed, non-secret placeholder password (matching this repo's existing `raise`/`raise` dev-credential convention), because Prisma migrations are static SQL checked into git and can't read a per-environment secret at apply time. This is fine for local dev and CI's ephemeral database, but **any real deployment must rotate this role's password out-of-band** (`ALTER ROLE raise_app WITH PASSWORD '...'`, via infra provisioning/secrets manager, not a migration) before go-live.
  - **Flag for CP12, sharper than "rotate the password": creating the role inside a migration at all is the wrong mechanism, not just a value that needs changing later.** Migrations are meant to be replayed — `prisma migrate deploy` re-runs this exact `CREATE ROLE ... PASSWORD 'raise_app'` (guarded by the `IF NOT EXISTS` check, so it won't error, but the statement, including the committed password, is still there and still gets parsed/executed every deploy) on every environment that ever runs this migration history, including a rotated production database if the whole migration set is ever replayed against it (a DR restore, a new read replica promoted to primary, standing up a second region). Role/credential provisioning belongs in an ops/infra step (Terraform, a one-time bootstrap script, a secrets-manager-driven `ALTER ROLE`) that migrations can assume has already happened — not inside version-controlled SQL that runs unattended and repeatedly. CP12 (launch readiness / security hardening) should extract this into that kind of step rather than treating "rotate the password" as the whole fix.
  - `users` and `customers` are deliberately **not** RLS-scoped: they're global identity tables (a staff user can hold memberships at more than one restaurant via `staff_memberships`; a customer isn't owned by any single restaurant). RLS applies to restaurant-owned data, not identity.

## CP3: validation library — Zod, not class-validator
- **Date**: 2026-09-14
- **Affects checkpoint(s)**: CP3 and any checkpoint validating request bodies after it
- **Decision**: Zod (`menuItemCreateSchema` / `menuItemUpdateSchema` in `@raise/shared-types`) via a hand-rolled `ZodValidationPipe`, following NestJS's own documented "Object schema validation" pipes recipe — not `class-validator`/`class-transformer`, NestJS's other officially documented option.
- **Why**: CP2's demo `MenuItemsController` took `Record<string, unknown>` and manually coerced fields — its own code review flagged this as exactly the kind of validation surface not to hand-roll. Confirmed via Context7 (`/nestjs/docs.nestjs.com`) that both Zod and class-validator are equally "official" NestJS patterns, so the deciding factor was reuse, not authority: the restaurant admin app (`apps/restaurant`) needs to validate the *same* rules (prepTimeMinutes positivity, allergens from a fixed set) client- and server-side, and a plain Zod schema is a runtime value any package can import and call `.safeParse()` on — a `class-validator` DTO class relies on `reflect-metadata` decorators that don't naturally cross into a Next.js client/server component tree the same way. One schema, defined once in `@raise/shared-types`, is what the API's `ZodValidationPipe` validates against AND what `apps/restaurant`'s Server Actions parse against for inline field errors — not two hand-written copies of "must be a positive integer" that could quietly drift apart.
- **Two fields get the correctness-critical treatment named in this checkpoint's instructions**: `prepTimeMinutes` is `z.number().int().positive()` with no `.default()` anywhere — CP6 computes `kitchen_start = arrival_eta - max(prepTimeMinutes) - buffer`, and a zero-defaulted dish makes the kitchen start late with nothing surfacing it as an error, so the schema rejects missing/zero/negative outright rather than coercing. `allergens` is `z.array(z.enum(ALLERGEN_TAGS))` — a fixed 9-tag set (matching the naming already used in `apps/api/prisma/seed.ts`), not a free string, so the admin UI can only ever offer structured checkbox selection and the API can never accept anything else. Both enforced identically on `create` and `update` (`.partial()` only makes a field optional to *omit*, not exempt from its rule when present).

## CP3: staff JWT storage — httpOnly cookie via a thin Next.js BFF, not localStorage
- **Date**: 2026-09-14
- **Affects checkpoint(s)**: CP3 (restaurant admin app) and any later checkpoint adding more restaurant-side screens (CP7, CP8)
- **Decision**: The staff JWT is set as an `httpOnly`, `Secure` (in production), `SameSite=Lax` cookie (`raise_staff_token`) on `apps/restaurant`'s own origin, written by a Server Action (`loginAction`) that calls the NestJS API server-side and never returns the token to client-side JS. Every subsequent call to `apps/api` (list/create/edit/disable a dish, `GET /auth/staff/me`) is made from a Server Component, Server Action, or the like — server-side, reading the cookie itself — never from the browser. `API_BASE_URL` (no `NEXT_PUBLIC_` prefix) is the only way `apps/restaurant` knows how to reach `apps/api`, and it is never exposed to a client bundle.
- **Why**: this is a real security decision with real consequences, not a default — considered explicitly rather than reaching for whichever is quickest:
  - **localStorage/sessionStorage**: trivially readable by any script that runs in the page, including a third-party dependency compromised after the fact (a supply-chain XSS) — and this token belongs to a role (`owner`) that can disable menu items and, from CP7 onward, will touch live operational data. A single XSS bug anywhere in `apps/restaurant`'s dependency tree would mean token exfiltration, full stop.
  - **httpOnly cookie set directly by the separate NestJS API** (a cross-site cookie, `apps/api` on one origin/port, `apps/restaurant` on another) was also considered and rejected: it would need `SameSite=None; Secure`, which is a materially weaker CSRF posture and increasingly restricted by browsers (Safari ITP, Chrome's third-party-cookie phase-out) — a cross-site cookie is exactly the pattern browsers are moving away from supporting reliably.
  - **The chosen shape (httpOnly cookie, same-site, set by a thin BFF layer in `apps/restaurant` itself)** means the browser only ever talks to its own origin; the JWT never crosses a network boundary the browser's JS can observe, and there's no cross-site cookie configuration to get wrong. The cost is that every data-fetching page/action in `apps/restaurant` must run server-side (Server Components/Actions) rather than client-side `fetch` — already true of everything built in this checkpoint (`requireStaffSession`, `apiFetch`), so no extra architecture was needed to get this property; it just had to be chosen deliberately instead of defaulted into.
  - `JWT_STAFF_SECRET`/`JWT_CUSTOMER_SECRET` (CP2) and this cookie decision together mean there is currently no code path anywhere in either frontend app that reads a staff or customer token from `localStorage`, a cookie readable by JS, or a URL parameter.

## CP3: a staff member's own restaurant is discovered via a new, narrowly-scoped RLS policy — not by embedding restaurant_id in the JWT
- **Date**: 2026-09-14
- **Affects checkpoint(s)**: CP3 (auth), any later checkpoint adding more `GET /auth/staff/me` consumers
- **Decision**: `apps/restaurant` needs to know which restaurant a freshly-logged-in staff member manages before it can call any restaurant-scoped endpoint — the staff JWT deliberately carries no `restaurantId` (CP2's design, since one user can hold memberships at more than one restaurant). Added `GET /auth/staff/me`, backed by a new migration (`cp3_staff_self_lookup`) adding a second, narrow permissive RLS policy to `staff_memberships`: a row is also visible if its `user_id` matches the caller's own verified identity (`app.current_user_id`, set only from a JWT's `sub` claim after verification — never client input).
- **Why this is not a repeat of the bypass CP2 deleted pre-merge**: the deleted `app.bypass_rls` policy granted visibility into *any* row on *every* tenant table the moment one flag was set — an unbounded escalation. This policy is bounded on both axes: it only ever applies to `staff_memberships` (no other table), and the session variable it checks can only ever equal the caller's own user id (never influenced by request input) — so the widest it can ever grant is "see your own membership rows," which is also all `GET /auth/staff/me` needs. `TenantPrismaService.forCurrentUser(userId, ...)` is the app-level half, mirroring `forRestaurant` exactly. Tested explicitly (`menu-items.e2e-spec.ts`): two different staff members' `/auth/staff/me` responses never cross-contaminate.
- Restaurant *names* for the response are fetched via a second, separately-scoped `forRestaurant` call per membership, not a join inside the `forCurrentUser` transaction — a join would need to read `restaurants` (which has its own, unrelated `tenant_isolation` policy keyed on `app.current_restaurant_id`, not set in this transaction) and would either return null silently or need a third carve-out policy. Two small scoped calls, each satisfying its own existing policy, was judged simpler and safer than adding a second exception to reason about.

## CP4 Part 8 Q1: ASR provider — Deepgram, HTTP transcribe (not a WebSocket streaming bridge), dev-stub default

- **Date**: 2026-09-15
- **Affects checkpoint(s)**: CP4 (intake pipeline); CP7 (first checkpoint that actually introduces this repo's WebSocket/realtime infrastructure)
- **Decision**: **Deepgram** (Nova-3, prerecorded/REST endpoint) is the production ASR provider, behind an `AsrProvider` interface (`transcribe(audio): Promise<{ transcript, confidenceLow }>`) that mirrors CP2's `OtpProvider` pattern exactly — same shape (interface + injection token + dev stub + real provider, selected by an env var, dev is the default). `ASR_PROVIDER=dev` (default) never calls a real ASR service; `ASR_PROVIDER=deepgram` requires `DEEPGRAM_API_KEY` and is opt-in only, exercised in a separate live-provider spec gated behind `RUN_LIVE_PROVIDER_TESTS=1` and skipped by default — CI never touches it, matching the Twilio-Verify precedent.
- **Why Deepgram over OpenAI Realtime or another provider**:
  - **Latency**: Deepgram's streaming/prerecorded Nova-3 tier returns transcripts in the low hundreds of milliseconds for short utterances, comfortably inside Part 5's ~1.5s speech-end-to-response-start budget when the LLM turn (Q2) is budgeted separately. OpenAI's Realtime API bundles ASR+LLM+voice into one socket, which can be faster end-to-end for an all-OpenAI stack, but this project's LLM (Q2) is Claude — coupling ASR to a different vendor's realtime socket than the one actually producing the dialogue would mean either discarding OpenAI's own LLM turn and just using it for transcription (paying for capability never used) or running two model vendors in the same turn. Keeping ASR and LLM as separate, independently-swappable stages (exactly Part 2.2's own pipeline diagram: ASR → dialogue manager) is the architecturally cleaner fit here, and Deepgram is a transcription-only specialist, not bundled.
  - **Cost**: Deepgram Nova-3 is priced per audio-second (streaming and prerecorded tiers both roughly $0.0043–0.0077/min at time of writing) — an 8-turn intake with ~15s of customer speech per turn (~2 minutes of audio total) costs low single-digit cents in ASR alone. OpenAI Realtime's audio-token pricing for a comparable session runs meaningfully higher because it's billing full multimodal model inference for the audio stream, not a dedicated transcription pass.
  - **Accent robustness**: Deepgram's Nova-3 is trained and explicitly marketed on broad accent/dialect coverage (a named benchmark focus, unlike Whisper-family models which are widely reported to have measurably higher error rates on non-US-English accents) — directly relevant here since RAISE's own product framing (hands-free, in-car, "the way you'd talk to a waiter") assumes real-world regional accents, not lab-clean American English.
  - **Fallback if Deepgram is unavailable (the "provider outage" unhappy path this checkpoint must handle)**: the pipeline does not hang or fail the turn. `AsrProvider.transcribe()` throwing (timeout, 5xx, missing/invalid key) is caught by `IntakeService`, which responds with an honest, non-blocking system turn ("Having trouble hearing you right now — you can keep going by typing") and the conversation continues in text mode over the *same* draft `Visit` and the *same* voice/type toggle already on screen — the toggle is not just a UX preference, it is the concrete fallback destination for ASR outage. No dead end, no silent retry loop, no fabricated transcript.
- **Why HTTP transcribe, not a persistent WebSocket streaming bridge, despite CP4's own "streaming ASR integration" deliverable wording**: checked this repo's actual infrastructure first, per CLAUDE.md Rule #1, rather than assuming a duplex streaming gateway was the only way to satisfy "streaming ASR" — **no WebSocket/realtime infrastructure exists anywhere in this codebase yet.** `docs/production-plan.md` Part 6 explicitly assigns "realtime updates via WebSocket" to CP7, not CP4; CP4 depends only on CP1/CP3. Building a bidirectional audio-streaming gateway now would be new infrastructure this checkpoint wasn't scoped for, and — more importantly — it would be **entirely unverifiable in this environment**: there is no browser, no microphone, and no live Deepgram key available here, so a WebSocket bridge could be written and would compile, but could never actually be run end-to-end before merge, which is a materially worse position than a correctly-scoped HTTP path that the offline test suite can actually exercise. Instead: the customer app captures one push-to-talk utterance at a time (`MediaRecorder`, already named as an acceptable pattern by Part 2.2's own "fallback push-to-talk" line) and posts the recorded clip to `POST /restaurants/:id/intake/:visitId/turn`; the backend calls Deepgram's REST transcription endpoint (still Nova-3, still low-latency for a short clip) rather than its websocket variant. ## CP4 Part 8 Q2: LLM + grounding — Claude Sonnet 5, tool-call-only menu access with live re-validation, no menu content ever in the prompt

- **Date**: 2026-09-15
- **Affects checkpoint(s)**: CP4 (intake pipeline) — the trust boundary CLAUDE.md Rule #4 requires flagging explicitly, not implementing around
- **Decision**: **Claude Sonnet 5** (`claude-sonnet-5`) drives the dialogue manager, via the Anthropic TypeScript SDK's manual tool-use loop (not the beta Tool Runner — see below), with **tool-call-only menu/table access plus mandatory live re-validation on every mutating tool call** as the grounding mechanism — not "prompt it to be careful," and not a single mechanism alone, but three independent layers stacked:
  1. **No menu content anywhere in the system prompt, ever.** The system prompt describes persona/behavior/rules only ("you have no memory of any dish, price, or table for this restaurant — you must call a tool to learn what's real, fresh, every turn; never state a name/price/availability that didn't come from a tool result in this same conversation"). There is nothing to hallucinate *from* for a specific restaurant's menu because the model is never given that restaurant's menu as context — every read (`list_available_menu_items`, `get_menu_item_details`, `list_open_tables`) is a live `TenantPrismaService.forRestaurant(...)` query executed at the moment of the tool call, same "no cache, no snapshot" discipline CP3 already proved for the admin API (see `menu-items.e2e-spec.ts`'s "immediately reflected" test, which this checkpoint's own grounding test mirrors for the conversational path).
  2. **State mutation only happens inside a tool handler that re-validates against the live table, never from the model's prose.** `add_order_item`/`update_order_item`/`propose_table` each take an id the model got from an earlier tool result in *this* conversation, and each independently re-queries Postgres for that id (`available: true`, correct `restaurantId`) **at call time** before writing anything — a turn where the model tries to add/propose an id that is no longer valid (disabled mid-conversation, wrong restaurant, never existed) gets a structured rejection result back, not a silent success, and the model is instructed to relay that honestly rather than retry silently. The client-visible/persisted order state (the `Visit`/`VisitItem` rows, returned in the API response and rendered by the UI) is only ever what a tool call actually wrote — the assistant's natural-language sentence is narration for the guest, never the source of truth, and the frontend renders the order summary from the structured draft, never by parsing the assistant's text.
  3. **Defense in depth below the application layer**: every tool's `input_schema` is declared with `strict: true` (Anthropic validates the shape server-side before the tool call even reaches this codebase — a model can't pass a malformed id past that layer), and `VisitItem.menuItemId` / `Visit.tableId` are real Postgres foreign keys to `MenuItem`/`Table` — even a bug in the tool-handler validation above could not make a nonexistent `menu_item_id` land in the database; the FK constraint rejects the insert.
  - **Why this makes a hallucinated dish structurally impossible, not just unlikely, and the test that proves it**: hallucination requires either (a) the model inventing a plausible name/price with nothing to check it against, or (b) a stale/invalid id slipping through into a written row. (a) is closed by having no menu data in context to begin with (layer 1) combined with the order state never being derived from the model's prose (layer 2) — even if the model's *narration* says something ungrounded, it cannot become an order line, because order lines are only ever created by a validated tool call. (b) is closed because the validation in layer 2 runs fresh on every single call, not once at conversation start — a `menuItemId` obtained from a tool result in turn 2 is re-checked in turn 6, not trusted as still valid. The checkpoint's required test (`intake.e2e-spec.ts`, "a dish disabled mid-conversation can no longer be accepted") drives this directly: seed an available dish, have the (deterministic dev) dialogue engine add it via the real `add_order_item` handler, disable the dish through CP3's real `PATCH .../menu-items/:id` mid-conversation, then attempt to add/re-add the same `menuItemId` — asserts the tool handler returns a rejection, no new `VisitItem` row is created referencing it, and the assistant's next turn is an honest "that's no longer available" message, not a silent success or a fabricated substitute.
  - **Why Sonnet 5, deliberately deviating from the `claude-api` skill's default ("always use claude-opus-5 unless told otherwise")**: checked the skill directly per Rule #1 before picking a model, and its own cost-optimization guidance is the reason for the deviation, not a preference override — it explicitly says chat/conversational, latency-sensitive, high-volume routes "often don't [benefit from higher effort/tier] and do well at low [effort]," while Opus-tier's premium is aimed at open-ended agentic/coding work. This dialogue manager is bounded, well-specified tool-calling over a small fixed toolset (list/add/remove/update/propose), not open-ended reasoning — exactly the shape the skill itself says doesn't need Opus. Sonnet 5 at `thinking: {type: "adaptive"}` / `output_config: {effort: "low"}` keeps per-turn latency and cost down against Part 5's ~1.5s NFR and this checkpoint's own "state the per-conversation cost estimate" requirement (see the PR description), while `strict: true` tool schemas keep tool-call reliability high regardless of tier. Documented explicitly, as the skill instructs, rather than silently picking a cheaper model without saying why.
  - **Manual loop, not the beta Tool Runner**: the Tool Runner (`client.beta.messages.toolRunner`) is convenient but is a beta SDK dependency, and this pipeline needs precise control over what happens *between* a rejected tool call and the next model turn (constructing the specific "that's no longer available" rejection payload, capping iterations per turn for latency, persisting each `ConversationTurn` with its `structuredDelta` as it happens) — the same "fewer moving parts for the same documented pattern" reasoning CP2 already applied when it chose a hand-rolled guard over `passport-jwt`.
  - **Dev/CI determinism**: `LlmDialogueEngine` interface, mirroring the ASR/OTP provider shape again. `LLM_PROVIDER=dev` (default) runs a small deterministic, keyword-driven dev engine that calls the *same real tool handlers* (same `TenantPrismaService`, same live-DB validation) as the real Claude engine — only "which words Claude would say next" is stubbed, not the grounding mechanism itself, which is provider-independent by design and must be proven regardless of which LLM is even configured. `LLM_PROVIDER=anthropic` (real Claude Sonnet 5, needs `ANTHROPIC_API_KEY`) is opt-in, exercised only behind `RUN_LIVE_PROVIDER_TESTS=1`, skipped by default — CI stays green with zero provider keys set, per this checkpoint's explicit requirement.

## CP4 Part 8 Q3: draft table-hold expiry and re-confirmation policy

- **Date**: 2026-09-15
- **Affects checkpoint(s)**: CP4 (draft-side policy + silence/timeout handling); CP5 (owns the real post-confirmation `Table.status = held` hold and its auto-expiry sweep — see Part 6's CP5 deliverables)
- **Decision**: A draft `Visit` gets a **15-minute inactivity window**, tracked by a new `Visit.draftExpiresAt` column that's refreshed to `now() + 15m` on every processed turn (voice or text). Table *proposals* made during CP4's conversation (`propose_table`) are informational/tentative only — CP4 never writes `Table.status`, and no table is actually locked against other guests during a draft. On expiry, the guest is never silently dropped or auto-confirmed: the next turn after `now() > draftExpiresAt` triggers an honest recap-and-reconfirm response instead of silently continuing ("That took a little while — here's what you had so far: [structured summary]. Still want to go ahead with this?"), and `draftExpiresAt` is refreshed again from that point.
- **Why 15 minutes, and why CP4 doesn't touch `Table.status`**: Part 6 is explicit that CP5, not CP4, owns "table hold logic on confirm; hold auto-expiry for abandoned drafts" — the *real*, booking-blocking hold is a post-confirmation concern. But Part 8 explicitly lists this as a Part-8 open question CP4 depends on, because CP4 still needs a policy number and a concrete behavior for its own "silence/timeout" unhappy path (this checkpoint's own requirement) and for Part 4.3's "customer never confirms (drafts abandoned)" edge case, even though the *enforcement* (releasing a real hold) is CP5's job. Building actual `Table.status = held` locking now, ahead of CP5, would be scope creep past what CP1's schema and CP5's own deliverable already anticipate — instead CP4 answers only what it's responsible for: how long a draft can sit silent before the pipeline stops treating it as "still mid-sentence" and starts treating it as "possibly abandoned, needs a fresh yes." 15 minutes is chosen as a plausible verbal-hold norm (long enough that a customer re-reading the menu or taking a phone call doesn't get treated as abandoned, short enough that a genuinely walked-away conversation doesn't sit ambiguously open for hours) — a product/business number, explicitly named as such per Part 8's own framing, and trivially adjustable (a single constant, not hard-coded through the logic) if restaurant interviews (`docs/user-research-plan.md`) later argue for a different window, the same "TBD pending interviews" treatment already given to the two decisions flagged at the top of this file.
- **What the guest sees**: never a dead end, never a silent restart, never an auto-confirmation. Either mid-window (normal turn) or post-expiry (recap-and-reconfirm) — the same honest, ask-again posture Part 4.3 requires for "nothing is booked until you say yes." Tested in `intake.e2e-spec.ts` by fast-forwarding a draft's `draftExpiresAt` into the past and asserting the next turn's response is the recap prompt, not a normal continuation, and that no `Visit.status` transition occurs.

## CP4 pre-merge fix: draft access was unauthenticated (IDOR) — closed with a draft-scoped capability token

- **Date**: 2026-09-15
- **Affects checkpoint(s)**: CP4 (intake pipeline); CP5 (about to hang phone verification and booking confirmation off the same `visitId` — this was the last cheap moment to fix it, per the report that triggered this fix)
- **The defect**: `IntakeController`'s two `:visitId` routes (`GET .../intake/:visitId`, `POST .../intake/:visitId/turn`) authorized on nothing but `restaurantId` — `tx.visit.findFirst({ where: { id: visitId, restaurantId }, ... })` matches any visit at that restaurant regardless of who's asking. `OptionalCustomerJwtGuard` resolved a `customerId` that was never checked against anything. Any holder of a `visitId` — which travels in a URL path (server logs, proxy logs, browser history, `Referer` headers) — could read another guest's full transcript (party size, arrival time, allergy flags, special needs) and mutate their draft order. `uuid(7)` entropy makes the id unguessable, not unleakable.

### Skills/tools enumerated before writing the fix (CLAUDE.md Rule #1)

- **`auth-failures`** (installed, real) — invoked before designing the token. Its checklist doesn't map cleanly onto a bearer *capability* token (no login flow, no password, no MFA question to answer — this isn't an identity credential), but its token-security items still applied directly: HS256 with a real secret (not hardcoded — `requireEnv('JWT_INTAKE_SECRET')`, dev-only placeholder in `.env.example` same as the other two JWT secrets), and an explicit expiry (see below) rather than an eternal token.
- **`broken-access-control`** (installed, real) — invoked after writing the guard, run as its own documented checklist against the actual code. Full report:

  - Object-level check: guard compares the verified token's {visitId, restaurantId} claims against the route params — not just "is this token valid," but "is this token valid FOR THIS resource." (intake-draft.guard.ts)

### The fix

- `IntakeTokenService` (`apps/api/src/auth/tokens/intake-token.service.ts`) mints a signed `intake-draft`-audience token with claims `{ visitId, restaurantId }`, its own secret `JWT_INTAKE_SECRET` — a third secret alongside `JWT_STAFF_SECRET`/`JWT_CUSTOMER_SECRET`, same "a leak of one can't forge the others" reasoning CP2 established for those two. Returned as `draftToken` in `IntakeTurnResponse`, from `POST .../intake/start` **and reissued fresh on every `POST .../intake/:visitId/turn` response** (mirroring `Visit.draftExpiresAt`'s own per-turn refresh).
- `IntakeDraftGuard` (`apps/api/src/auth/guards/intake-draft.guard.ts`) is applied via method-level `@UseGuards` on `GET :visitId` and `POST :visitId/turn` only — never on `start`, which stays guest-first per Part 8 Q4 (no login wall to begin talking). It verifies signature + audience, then requires the token's `visitId`/`restaurantId` claims to match the route params exactly; any failure 404s. `OptionalCustomerJwtGuard` is untouched — its `customerId` linkage is a separate, orthogonal concern (which guest this draft eventually belongs to once verified), not an authorization mechanism for the draft itself.
- **Token TTL matches the draft inactivity window (15 min) and is reissued every turn — a known, accepted tradeoff, not an oversight.** Because the token's `exp` and `Visit.draftExpiresAt` are deliberately the same window, a guest who is *actually* silent for the full 15 real minutes (not the DB-column-only fast-forward the timeout test uses) will find their held token has *also* really expired by the time they return — `IntakeDraftGuard` then 404s their next request before it ever reaches `IntakeService`'s own recap-and-reconfirm logic for that specific case. In every case that actually matters in practice (a guest who is still actively exchanging turns, however slowly, within the window), the token is refreshed on each turn right alongside the draft itself, so this only bites a guest who leaves the tab open and does *nothing at all* for a full 15 real minutes — a corner case CP4's own recap feature was built for and this fix narrows slightly. Recorded here per Rule #4 rather than silently resolved; a future pass could special-case "expired-but-otherwise-valid intake token" to still reach the recap flow (verify ignoring expiration, then require the caller send whatever fresh state comes back) if this corner case turns out to matter in practice.
- Also fixed while in this file: `IntakeService.processTurn` used to return `allTurns.slice(-2)` as "the two new turns" — assumed exactly two rows and no concurrent interleaving. Now returns the exact `ConversationTurn` rows this call created, by reference (no re-query, no position-based guessing). This also fixed a second, related bug the same code shape was hiding: the silence/timeout and LLM-outage response paths were silently dropping the customer's own just-persisted turn from the response (only the system reply was ever returned on those paths) — `respondWithSystemTurn` now takes the preceding turns to echo back explicitly.
- `apps/customer`'s `IntakeChat` holds `draftToken` in component state only (never `localStorage`, never a URL) and sends it as `Authorization: Bearer <token>` on every turn; the two Next.js Route Handlers relay the header through to `apps/api` verbatim without inspecting or storing it.

### The fix

- `apps/api/src/intake/draft-policy.ts` now exposes two independently-configurable windows instead of one shared constant: `getDraftInactivityWindowMs()` (business staleness, still 15 minutes by default) and `getDraftTokenTtlMs()`/`getDraftTokenTtlSeconds()` (the token's own cryptographic ceiling, **2 hours by default** — deliberately not a second business-meaningful number, just a grace period comfortably longer than the business window). Both are read fresh on every call via optional `DRAFT_INACTIVITY_WINDOW_MS_OVERRIDE`/`DRAFT_TOKEN_TTL_MS_OVERRIDE` env vars (test-only, same convention as `OTP_DEV_FIXED_CODE`) rather than cached at module load, so a test can exercise real short windows without mocking the system clock.
- **Why 2 hours, not unbounded**: a token with no ceiling at all would be strictly worse hygiene than the current design even though the underlying draft data itself isn't deleted on any particular schedule — an unboundedly-valid bearer credential is a bigger, permanent standing liability if it ever leaks through a channel other than the URL-path leak this whole fix exists to close (a shared screen, a copy-pasted support message, a compromised dependency reading `localStorage` if this app is ever changed to persist it there). 2 hours is chosen the same way Part 8 Q3's 15-minute business window was chosen — a plausible, explicitly-reasoned product number (long enough that a guest who steps away for a phone call, a long red light, or a conversation with the table is never actually locked out; short enough that a token is not a de-facto permanent credential), not derived from a formula, and trivially adjustable if restaurant interviews argue otherwise — same "TBD pending interviews" treatment as the two decisions flagged at the top of this file.
- **What a guest experiences at each boundary**: within the 15-minute business window, a normal turn. Between 15 minutes and 2 hours of silence, the next turn is authenticated successfully and receives the recap-and-reconfirm message (Part 8 Q3, now actually reachable) — never a silent continuation, never an auto-confirm, matching Part 4.3's "nothing is booked until you say yes." Past 2 hours, the token itself has expired and `IntakeDraftGuard` 404s — the guest's browser tab, if still open, would need to start a fresh conversation via the same restaurant link; the old draft row is not deleted (still available for a future admin/observability pass, matching this repo's general "audit trail, don't destroy" posture) but is no longer reachable through that stale token. This is a deliberate, bounded backstop, not an accident — and is now a materially rarer, more defensible edge case than the pre-fix design, where it was the *common* case (any pause over 15 minutes) rather than the rare one.
- Every response from `start`/`turn` still reissues a fresh token on its own new 2-hour clock (unchanged behavior from the original fix) — an actively-continuing conversation never approaches either ceiling regardless of which one is shorter.

### The trust boundary

`VisitsService.confirm` is the **only** method anywhere in this codebase that may write `Visit.status = 'confirmed'` — enforced not just by convention but by a dedicated static test (`visits.e2e-spec.ts`'s `TRUST BOUNDARY` test) that scans every source file under `apps/api/src` (excluding `visits.service.ts` itself, generated code, and specs) for the literal pattern `status: 'confirmed'` and fails if it finds one anywhere else. This is deliberately a structural check, not a behavioral one — a *future* checkpoint could add a new write path that never gets exercised by this suite's other tests; this one fails the moment that happens, regardless of where.

- **Idempotency mechanism**: an atomic conditional `updateMany({ where: { id, restaurantId, status: 'draft' }, data: { status: 'confirmed', ... } })`. A second call for an already-`confirmed` visit is detected by a pre-check (`if (visit.status === 'confirmed') return { ...alreadyConfirmed: true }`) *before* even entering the transaction — no new database write happens at all for the common double-tap/retry case, and the same booking is returned both times. If two calls for the same visit race past the pre-check simultaneously, the conditional `updateMany` itself only lets one through (`count === 0` on the loser), which is caught (`VisitClaimLostError`), re-resolved by re-reading the now-confirmed row, and returned as the same idempotent success if the caller is the same customer, or a `409` if something else changed the row.
- **Concurrency mechanism — explicitly not a check-then-write**: the table claim uses the identical pattern (`tx.table.updateMany({ where: { id, restaurantId, status: 'free' }, data: { status: 'held' } })`), inside the *same* transaction as the visit-status claim, so either both succeed or neither does (a table-claim failure throws, which rolls back the visit-status write too — Prisma's interactive `$transaction` callback). Two guests racing for the same table each attempt this `UPDATE`; Postgres's own row-level locking on the `UPDATE ... WHERE` statement serializes them — the second one's `WHERE status = 'free'` re-evaluates against the now-`'held'` row and matches zero rows. No `SELECT FOR UPDATE`, no elevated isolation level, no application-level lock — confirmed correct against Context7's own documented Prisma pattern for exactly this class of problem (see above), and proven with a real race: two genuinely concurrent HTTP requests (`Promise.all`, not sequential `await`s) against two different draft visits proposing the same table.
- **What releases the table hold if confirmation never completes**: nothing needs to, and that's a deliberate consequence of the design, not an oversight. `Table.status` only ever becomes `'held'` inside this one atomic transaction, at the moment of a *successful* confirm — CP4's own draft-phase table proposal (Part 8 Q3) never touches `Table.status` at all (documented in the CP4 entries above). An abandoned draft — one that never reaches a successful confirm, for any reason — therefore never created a real hold in the first place, so `docs/checkpoints/CP05-confirmation-booking.md`'s "abandoned drafts release their table hold after the configured window" is satisfied by construction: there is nothing to release. What happens to a table hold *after* a successful confirm if the guest then no-shows or cancels is explicitly out of this checkpoint's scope — that's CP6 (timing engine)/a future checkpoint's job, and is called out here rather than silently assumed away.
- **Identity binding**: `Visit.customerId` starts `null` under the guest-first design (Part 8 Q4) and is bound to whichever verified customer's confirm call actually wins the atomic claim — see the `broken-access-control` report above for why a second, different customer can never confirm an already-linked (or concurrently-being-linked) draft.
- **Every transition is recorded with who/when/how**: `VisitStatusEvent` gained `actorType`/`actorId`/`mechanism` columns (nullable — CP1-CP4 never wrote this table at all; the full transition log is a CP6 deliverable per Part 6, and CP5 is the first checkpoint to populate any of it, for the one transition it owns). `occurredAt` was already there for "when."
